\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Amorphous heavy-metal alloys are of growing interest in spintronics, where
their ability to generate spin--orbit torque from a structural, rather than a
chemical, order makes them attractive spin-current sources. Ion-implanted
amorphous Pt(P), for example, exhibits a giant spin Hall effect and
spin--orbit torque that can exceed those of crystalline heavy metals
\cite{shashank2025}, and comparably large spin Hall effects are reported in
amorphous binary alloys and in amorphous tungsten
\cite{fritz2018,liao2019}. Amorphous materials more broadly are emerging
across electronics applications as their properties are tuned by disorder
rather than by composition alone \cite{shi2025,madanchi2024}. Because these
properties depend on the atomic-scale structure, knowing {\em when} a
candidate system becomes amorphous --- and over what composition range --- is
central to designing such films. Designing an amorphous host for spin Hall
conductivity is itself a challenge: the transport tensor must be averaged
over the many inequivalent disordered configurations that represent the
amorphous state \cite{turek2019,shashank2025}. The concentration that matters
for a device is not simply the one that maximizes the spin Hall conductivity:
because the write energy of a spin-orbit-torque device scales with the ratio
of the resistivity to the square of the spin Hall angle,
$\xi = \rho/\theta_{\mathrm{SH}}^2 = \sigma_{xx}/\sigma_{\mathrm{SH}}^2$
\cite{yoon2025}, the useful composition is the one that minimizes this
power-consumption factor, balancing the spin Hall conductivity against the
in-plane conductivity and resistivity.

Predicting structure across that regime is, however, costly. Generating an
amorphous configuration is conventionally done by first-principles melt-quench
molecular dynamics, which is prohibitively expensive at the level of accuracy
needed for a multi-element alloy \cite{meltquench2026,madanchi2024}, and the
tens of thousands of reference calculations that a general machine-learned
interatomic potential demands are out of reach for exploratory screening
\cite{activemlp2020}. Estimating a disorder-averaged transport property, such
as the spin Hall conductivity of a model amorphous alloy
\cite{turek2019}, multiplies this cost further, because the value must be
averaged over many inequivalent structures. Evolutionary and
machine-learning-assisted sampling have lowered the barrier
\cite{artrith2018,nahas2016,valladares2011}, but a practical route to predict
the amorphous onset of an interstitial alloy from first principles remains
open.

Here we take a cheap, reference-anchored global-optimization screening
approach: rather than melting and quenching, we model the amorphous state as
the low-energy basin around a chosen crystalline reference and enumerate that
basin with a surrogate-guided search
\cite{gofee2020,gofee2022,agox2022}. The crystalline reference is a physical
choice the user controls --- unique for a host with a single stable phase, and
a genuine design choice for a polymorphic one. Amorphization is then confirmed
structurally with the Steinhardt $q_6$ bond-orientational order parameter
\cite{steinhardt1983}, cross-checked against simulated X-ray diffraction.
Because the surrogate guides the search, the basin is sampled at a small
fraction of the cost of melt-quench dynamics \cite{artrith2018}.

In this work, we successfully demonstrated that a cheap, reference-anchored
global-optimization screening identifies amorphous minima in an interstitial
alloy, enabling the prediction of the structural amorphization onset of Pt(P)
at 20--30~at.\% P and revealing its saturation with concentration and its mild
promotion by cell expansion, consistent with the experimental onset of
ion-implanted Pt(P) \cite{shashank2025}. This paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.~II, we present the reference-anchored global-optimization method and
the crystallinity verification. In Sec.~III, we first discuss the
computational cost and search dynamics of the screening, and subsequently
introduce the crystallinity of the Pt(P) systems and their comparison with
experiment. We summarize our work in Sec.~IV.
